​CHAPTER 1​ ​The Data Scientist​

​Master the real number system to classify and compute with any number​

​What You'll Learn​

​•​ ​Distinguish between rational and irrational numbers​ ​•​ ​Locate real numbers on the number line​ ​•​ ​Approximate irrational numbers​ ​•​ ​Perform operations with rational and irrational numbers​

​Why This Matters​

​Data scientists work with numbers constantly—from tiny decimals to massive datasets.​ ​Some numbers are clean and exact (like 0.5 or 3/4), while others go on forever without​ ​repeating (like π or √2). Understanding the difference helps you choose the right level of​ ​precision for calculations that matter.​

​Section 1.1: Classifying Numbers​

​The real number system organizes all numbers into categories based on their properties.​

​The Number Hierarchy​

​Natural Numbers (N): 1, 2, 3, 4, ... (counting numbers)​

​Whole Numbers (W): 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, ... (natural numbers + zero)​

​Integers (Z): ..., -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, ... (whole numbers + negatives)​

​Rational Numbers (Q): Any number that can be written as a fraction a/b where a and b​ ​are integers and b ≠ 0​

​Irrational Numbers: Numbers that CANNOT be written as a fraction—their decimals go​ ​on forever without repeating​

​Real Numbers (R): All rational and irrational numbers combined​

​Rational: Can be written as a/b | Irrational: Cannot be written​

​as a/b​

​Recognizing Rational Numbers​

​A number is rational if its decimal representation either:​

​•​ ​Terminates (ends): 0.25, 3.5, -2.125​ ​•​ ​Repeats: 0.333..., 0.142857142857..., 2.166666...​

​WORKED EXAMPLE: Classifying 0.375​

​Recognizing Irrational Numbers​

​Common irrational numbers:​

​•​ ​π ≈ 3.14159265358979... (never repeats, never ends)​ ​•​ ​√2 ≈ 1.41421356237... (square root of any non-perfect square)​ ​•​ ​e ≈ 2.71828182845... (Euler's number)​

​Section 1.3: Operations with Real Numbers​

​When you add, subtract, multiply, or divide real numbers, the result type depends on​ ​what you started with.​

​Key Rules​

​Rational + Rational = Rational (always)​

​Rational × Rational = Rational (always)​

​Irrational + Irrational = Can be either (√2 + √2 = 2√2 is irrational, but √2 + (-√2) = 0 is​ ​rational)​

​Rational × Irrational = Irrational (usually, except when multiplying by 0)​

​Irrational × Irrational = Can be either (√2 × √2 = 2 is rational, but √2 × √3 = √6 is​ ​irrational)​

​Section 2.1: Properties of Integer Exponents​

​The Exponent Rules​

​Product Rule: aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ​

​Quotient Rule: aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ​

​Power Rule: (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐˣⁿ​

​Zero Exponent: a⁰ = 1 (when a ≠ 0)​

​Negative Exponent: a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ​

​WORKED EXAMPLE: Simplify 2⁴ × 2³​

​Step 1:​​Apply product rule: add exponents with same​​base​ ​Step 2:​​2⁴ × 2³ = 2⁴⁺³ = 2⁷​ ​Step 3:​​Calculate: 2⁷ = 128​

​WORKED EXAMPLE: Simplify 5⁶ ÷ 5²​

​Step 1:​​Apply quotient rule: subtract exponents​ ​Step 2:​​5⁶ ÷ 5² = 5⁶⁻² = 5⁴​ ​Step 3:​​Calculate: 5⁴ = 625​

​What You'll Learn​

​Why This Matters​

​Space scientists work with numbers that are impossibly large (93 million miles to the​ ​Sun) and impossibly small (atoms measured in billionths of a meter). Scientific notation​ ​makes these numbers manageable—and comparable. It's the language of science.​

​Section 3.1: Understanding Scientific Notation​

​Scientific Notation: a × 10ⁿ where 1 ≤ a < 10​

​Large numbers: positive exponent (move decimal left)​

​Small numbers: negative exponent (move decimal right)​

​WORKED EXAMPLE: Write 93,000,000 in scientific notation​

​Step 1:​​Move decimal until you have a number between​​1 and 10: 9.3​ ​Step 2:​​Count places moved: 7 places to the left​ ​Step 3:​​Write: 9.3 × 10⁷​

​WORKED EXAMPLE: Write 0.00045 in scientific notation​

​Step 1:​​Move decimal until you have a number between​​1 and 10: 4.5​ ​Step 2:​​Count places moved: 4 places to the right​ ​Step 3:​​Since original number is small, exponent is​​negative: 4.5 × 10⁻⁴​

​Section 5.3: Slope-Intercept Form​

​y = mx + b​

​m = slope (rate of change)​

​b = y-intercept (starting value)​

​Finding Slope​

​slope = (y₂ - y₁) / (x₂ - x₁) = rise / run​

​WORKED EXAMPLE: Find slope through (2, 5) and (6, 13)​

​Step 1:​​Use slope formula: m = (13 - 5) / (6 - 2)​ ​Step 2:​​Calculate: m = 8 / 4 = 2​

​Section 6.1: Understanding Systems​

​A system of equations is two or more equations with the same variables.​

​The solution is the point (x, y) that makes BOTH equations true.​

​Types of Solutions​

​One solution: Lines intersect at one point​

​No solution: Lines are parallel (never intersect)​

​Infinite solutions: Lines are the same (overlap completely)​

​a² + b² = c²​

​Where a and b are the legs (sides that form the right angle)​
