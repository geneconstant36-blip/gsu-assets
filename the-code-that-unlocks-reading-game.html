Why We Play

We play because reading about a thing and being able to do a thing are not the same, and anyone who has nodded along to an explanation and then frozen at the page already knows it. A game is not decoration on top of the lesson. It is the moment the lesson stops being someone else's knowledge and becomes yours — because you moved the dial, you heard the vowel change, you split the word yourself. Nobody learns to drive from a pamphlet.

And we play because the stakes are real. A person who cannot decode the letter from the school, the label on the bottle, or the clause in the lease is not merely inconvenienced — they are dependent, and dependence is the thing this university exists to end. So the bar here is honest. You will not be handed a certificate for showing up, and you will not be told you did well when you did not. What you will be given, every single time you fall short, is the exact chapter that fixes it. That is the trade: we will never flatter you, and we will never abandon you.

Reference

The Phonics Dictionary

Sixteen words that do all the heavy lifting in the sound code, in plain English, exactly as the book defines them.

PhonemeA single unit of sound in speech. The word cat has three: /k/ /a/ /t/. Phonemes are heard, not seen.
GraphemeThe written unit that spells a phoneme. It may be one letter or several — the /sh/ sound is one phoneme spelled with the two-letter grapheme sh.
Alphabetic principleThe understanding that English spelling maps sounds to letters in a consistent, learnable way, even when a sound is spelled with more than one letter.
CVC wordA consonant-vowel-consonant word such as cat, bed, or sun. The most reliable street in the code and the usual place to start.
Silent eA final e that makes no sound of its own and changes the vowel before it from short to long. Hop becomes hope; cap becomes cape.
Vowel teamTwo or more letters working together to spell one vowel sound, as in rain, seat, or boat. Some teams are position-sensitive: ai works inside a word, ay at the end.
Consonant blendTwo or three consonants whose separate sounds are all still heard, as in stop or blend. A blend keeps its sounds.
DigraphTwo letters that together make one new sound, as in ship, chin, or thin. A digraph replaces its sounds rather than keeping them.
R-controlled vowelA vowel whose sound is changed by a following r — /ar/ in car, /or/ in for, /er/ in her. For many speakers bird and turn share the /er/ sound.
DiphthongA vowel sound that glides from one mouth position to another, as /oi/ in boil and /ou/ in cow.
SchwaThe relaxed vowel of an unstressed syllable, the /uh/ heard in about, pencil, and sofa. It is why a word can be decoded correctly and still sound slightly wrong.
BlendingPushing separate sounds together into a whole word — the move that turns /k/ /a/ /t/ into cat.
SegmentingBreaking a spoken word back into its separate sounds. The reverse of blending, and the move that makes spelling possible.
SyllableA word part built around one vowel sound. Counting vowel sounds, not vowel letters, is what counts syllables correctly.
Synthetic phonicsTeaching the code part to whole: learn the sounds, then blend them into words. The approach this book follows.
DecodingTurning print into speech by running the code, rather than guessing from pictures, context, or the shape of a word.

Questions

Frequently Asked

What is The Sound Climb?

The Sound Climb is a free phonics game in two halves. The first half is a bench of six live instruments — you move a dial or flip a switch and watch, and hear, what happens to a word. Nothing is scored there; it exists so the code makes sense in your hands before anyone asks you a question about it. The second half is an endless climb of practical tasks that get harder as you rise. Every task is built fresh by the page, so you can play a hundred times and never meet the same run twice.

Does it make sound, and can I turn it off?

Yes to both. Phonics is about sound, so the instruments speak whole words aloud using the voice already built into your device — press the silent e switch and you hear hop become hope. There is a mute button at the top of the bench and beside every instrument, and the whole page works perfectly with sound off. We deliberately never speak isolated letter sounds on their own, because synthetic voices pronounce bare phonemes badly and would teach you the wrong thing.

Is it really free, and do I need an account?

The game is free forever. There is no account, no sign-up, no email address, and no password. The page signs you in anonymously so it can remember your best climb and issue a certificate serial number, but it never learns who you are. The complete twelve-chapter book behind the game is also free to read on this site and free to have read aloud to you. The Kindle edition is $3.99 on Amazon, and those sales are what fund the Foundation.

I am an adult who never learned this. Is this for me?

It is built for you specifically. The book says plainly that the brain does not age out of learning patterns, and that what adults usually lack is not intelligence but a coherent map of the code nobody ever gave them. Nothing here is decorated for children, nothing is patronising, and nothing announces your level to anyone. You can work the entire bench without answering a single scored question, for as long as you like, and nobody sees any of it.

How do I earn the Certificate of Comprehension?

You reach Level 10 of the climb in a single run without losing all three strikes. That means clearing fifty tasks while the difficulty rises and the timer shrinks. Every level you clear hands one strike back, so a single mistake will not end your run, but a habit of them will. There is no way to guess your way there — tasks are generated fresh, they draw on six different skills, and the answer choices are deliberately length-matched so nothing gives itself away.

What happens if I do not make it?

You get told the truth, and then you get help. Global Sovereign University does not hand out certificates for showing up, because too many people were passed forward without ever learning the material and that is the failure this university exists to answer. When a run ends short, the page shows exactly which of the six skills cost you the most tasks, names the chapter of the book that teaches it, and links straight into that chapter and back to the instrument that drills it.

Is everything on the climb taught somewhere free?

Yes, and it matters enough to say plainly: every single thing the climb asks you is taught free on this site. All twelve chapters are readable at no cost in the GSU Reading Room, an audio reader will read them aloud, and the six instruments drill the same patterns by hand. We never test material we have not first given you. If a task ever asks something you cannot find in the free book, that is our mistake and not yours.

GENO has memorized the entire book — all twelve chapters, front to back, not merely a sample chapter. You can ask him about any paragraph in it, in any of the 83 languages he speaks, and he will answer from the text itself.
