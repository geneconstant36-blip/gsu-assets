Why We Play

We play because reading about a thing and being able to do a thing are not the same, and everyone who has ever nodded along to an explanation and then frozen at the keyboard already knows it. A game is not a decoration on top of the lesson. It is the moment the lesson stops being someone else's knowledge and becomes yours — because you moved the slider, you built the path, you picked the wrong address and watched what it cost. Nobody learns to drive from a pamphlet.

And we play because the stakes are real. A person who cannot find their own tax document, spot a fake bank message, or explain why the computer got slow is not merely inconvenienced — they are dependent, and dependence is the thing this university exists to end. So the bar here is honest. You will not be handed a certificate for showing up, and you will not be told you did well when you did not. What you will be given, every single time you fall short, is the exact page that fixes it. That is the trade: we will never flatter you, and we will never abandon you.

Reference

The Working Dictionary

Sixteen words that do all the heavy lifting in computer literacy, in plain English, exactly as the book defines them.

RAMRandom Access Memory — the computer's short-term working space, like counter space in a kitchen. Temporary, and it clears when the machine shuts down. Not the same thing as storage.
StorageThe long-term filing cabinet where files stay after shutdown. Storage keeps things; RAM only works on them.
PacketA small labeled chunk of data. Messages are chopped into packets that can travel different routes and be reassembled at the far end — which is why the internet survives broken cables.
DNSThe Domain Name System, the internet's phone book. You type a name, it returns the number. When DNS fails your connection can be fine while no site will load.
IP addressA numerical address identifying a device on a network, such as 192.168.1.25. Names are for people; addresses are for networks.
RouterThe traffic director. At home it lets many devices share one connection; out on the internet, routers decide which way to send your packets next.
ModemThe translator between your home and your internet provider. The router directs traffic; the modem speaks the provider's dialect.
ServerA computer built to serve many people at once rather than one person at a screen. The cloud is not up — it is away, on servers in a data center.
SSIDThe name of a wireless network — the label on the front door. A name is not security. A scammer can copy a coffee shop's network name exactly.
File extensionThe letters after the period in a filename, such as .pdf or .docx, telling the computer what kind of file it is. Renaming an extension changes the label, never the file.
EncryptionScrambling information so anyone intercepting it sees nonsense. A padlock means the conversation is private, not that the other party is honest.
PhishingA fake message built to make you log in, pay, or hand something over. The criminal does not want to break your password — they want you to type it on a page they control.
Credential stuffingTaking passwords stolen in one breach and trying them everywhere else. It works because so many people reuse a single password.
Two-factor authenticationA second proof beyond the password, such as a rotating code from an authenticator app. Even a stolen password will not get someone in.
PassphraseA long password made of several words you can picture. Fourteen to twenty characters or more, and never built from facts a stranger could look up.
MalwareHarmful software arriving by link, attachment, download, or fake update. It can spy, steal passwords, or hold your files for ransom.

Questions

Frequently Asked

What is The Confidence Climb?

The Confidence Climb is a free computer literacy game in two halves. The first half is a bench of six live instruments — you drag a slider or flip a switch and watch what happens inside a computer, a network, a file path, or a password. Nothing is scored there; it exists so you understand the idea before anyone asks you about it. The second half is the climb itself, an infinite ladder of practical tasks that get harder as you rise. Every task is built fresh by the page, so you can play a hundred times and never get the same run twice.

Is it really free, and do I need an account?

The game is free forever and always will be. There is no account, no sign-up form, no email address, and no password. The page signs you in anonymously so it can save your best climb and issue a certificate serial number, but it never learns who you are. The complete book behind the game, Computer Literacy Unlocked, is also free to read on this site and free to have read aloud to you. If you would rather own the Kindle edition, it is $3.99 on Amazon, and that sale is what funds the Foundation.

What do the six instruments actually do?

Each instrument turns one chapter of the book into something you can operate with your hands. The Memory Bench lets you add browser tabs until the machine runs out of counter space, so you can see why a computer slows down. The Packet Run sends a message across the world and shows you the lag. The Path Builder assembles a real file path and exposes the hidden-extension trick scammers use. The Passphrase Forge shows how long a password would survive. The Address Bar takes a web address apart so you can find the real owner. The Layer Ladder hands you a broken connection and asks you to find the layer that failed.

How do I earn the Certificate of Comprehension?

You reach Level 10 of the climb in a single run without losing all three strikes. Every level you clear hands one strike back, so a single mistake will not end your run — but a habit of them will. That means clearing fifty tasks while the difficulty keeps rising and the timer keeps shrinking. There is no way to guess your way there — the tasks are generated fresh, they draw on six different skills, and the answer choices are deliberately written to be the same length so nothing gives itself away. When you clear Level 10 the page issues a unique serial number, records it in a public registry, and lets you download the certificate as an image.

What happens if I do not make it?

You get told the truth, and then you get help. Global Sovereign University does not hand out certificates for showing up, because the reason this university exists is that too many people were passed forward without ever learning the material. So when a run ends short, the page shows you exactly which of the six skills cost you the most tasks, names the chapter of the book that teaches it, and gives you a direct link into that chapter and back to the instrument that drills it. Then you climb again. Nothing is locked and nothing is lost.

Can anyone check that my certificate is real?

Yes. Every certificate carries a serial number beginning with GSU-CLU, and that serial is written to a public registry the moment it is issued. Anyone — an employer, a caseworker, a family member — can type the serial into the verification box on this page and see the subject, the tier, the score, and the date it was earned. The record cannot be edited after it is written, which is what makes it worth having.

Is everything on the climb actually taught somewhere free?

Yes, and this matters enough that we will say it plainly: every single thing the climb asks you is taught free on this site. The complete twenty-chapter book is readable at no cost in the GSU Reading Room, an audio reader will read it aloud to you, and the six instruments on this page drill the same ideas by hand. We never test material we have not first given you. If a task ever asks something you cannot find in the free book, that is our mistake, not yours.
