The Grade Level Check

Ninety percent of parents believe their child is on grade level.
About thirty percent are.

Both numbers are measured. Neither is disputed. The distance between them is the most consequential thing almost nobody has told you.

How a report card can be honest and still be wrong

Ask parents of an eighth grader how their child is doing, and roughly nine in ten say at or above grade level. They are not guessing. They are reading the report card, and the report card says B.

Then look at national testing, where students sit the same questions and the results are compared against what an eighth grader is expected to know. Roughly three in ten are proficient in reading. Roughly three in ten in mathematics.

~9 in 10parents who believe their eighth grader is at or above grade level
~3 in 10eighth graders who test proficient in reading or mathematics

Parent confidence from national survey research. Proficiency from the National Assessment of Educational Progress, the federal testing programme known as the Nation's Report Card.

Something in the middle is translating one into the other. Researchers have a name for it: B-flation.

Middle school grades usually blend two different things. One is whether the student understands the material. The other is whether the student turned work in on time, behaved in class, participated in the group, and followed directions. Both matter. But they get added together and reported as a single letter, and the second one is much easier to satisfy than the first.

A student who is organised, polite, and punctual — and who cannot read a chapter and tell you what it argued — will earn B's for years. Every one of those B's is a true statement about compliance and a false impression about mastery.

The report card is not lying. It is answering a different question than the one you asked.

Why this matters more than it sounds

A gap you know about is a problem. A gap you do not know about is a trajectory. The student moves to the next grade, and the next, on material they never secured — and each year the work assumes the previous year held.

By the time it surfaces, it surfaces as something else. A failed placement exam. A course that has to be repeated. A career door that quietly does not open. And the person on the receiving end usually concludes they are not smart, because nobody ever showed them the actual measurement.

They were not failed by their intelligence. They were failed by a measurement that never arrived.

So here is the measurement. Free, and nobody is watching.

This check gives you a straight answer about reading and mathematics: the highest grade level at which the answers hold up. It takes about ten minutes per subject.

Everything happens inside your browser. No account, no email, no sign-up, and no results sent anywhere — not to a school, not to us. There is no server call in this page at all. That is deliberate, and it is not only for children.

Plenty of adults carry a private suspicion that something was missed. They can find out here, alone, and start closing it the same day — with nobody informed and nothing on a record. If that is you, this was built for you too.

What this check is, and what it is not. It is a screener. It is not a standardized test: it is not norm-referenced against a national sample and it has not been psychometrically validated. It does not satisfy any state's homeschool assessment requirement. If your state requires annual testing, use an approved provider — this tells you where you stand before you get there, which is a different and useful thing.

A result here is a signal, not a diagnosis. A tired child, an unfamiliar question format, or a bad ten minutes can move it. If the result surprises you in either direction, take it again another day before you act on it.
